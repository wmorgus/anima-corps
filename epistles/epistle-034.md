Epistle 034 — The Cite Must Carry Its Content
Topic: A citation must carry a succinct honest description of what it cites — enough that a reader cannot claim good-faith reading and still answer in bad faith. The bare artifact ID protects the sender only.

§3.11–§3.13 bind the sender. Citation as schema (§3.11): every evidentiary claim anchored to an artifact ID. Narrative inherits the epistemic status of what it cites (§3.12). Confidence without citation is laundered (§3.13). All three protect the *citer*: claim is anchored, confidence is traceable, the synthesis is tethered to something real.

What they leave open: the *receiver*.

The bare artifact ID is sender-protection only. "§11.4" is a well-formed citation. It satisfies §3.11. It tethers the claim. And it hands the reader nothing to grip. The reader sees the cite, nods, answers from their own assumed reading of §11.4 — which may be partial, may be wrong, may be a convenient misreading. The citer is formally honest: they cited. The reader is formally honest: they read the cite. The communication fails anyway. Neither party lied. The gap is between the ID and its content, and both parties can hide in it.

The live failure: claim about the information poet, cited "§11.4 and §11.5" — IDs only. A well-anchored claim that left the reader with nothing to read. Good anchoring, zero legibility.

**The obligation extends to the receiver.** A citation must carry a succinct honest description of the cited content. Not the ID alone — the ID plus a caveman gloss of what it says. The gloss closes the gap. After it, the reader cannot claim good-faith engagement with the cite and answer in bad faith: the content was in the message. To miss it is to not have read; to answer against it is bad faith you can no longer launder as misunderstanding. The description converts "you should have looked it up" into "it was in front of you."

This is the receiver-side of §3.13's logic. §3.13 stops the *sender* laundering confidence past the citation field. §034 stops the *receiver* laundering a bad-faith answer past the bare ID. Same disease, other end of the wire.

Rules out: treating a bare artifact ID as a complete citation. Treating §3.11's sender-anchoring as sufficient epistemic hygiene for communication — anchoring tethers the claim to the record; it does not make the claim legible across the wire. A claim can be perfectly anchored and still arrive opaque.

Where this sits among the residuals: not §3.15 (motivation — can't enforce the cycle was honest) and not §3.16 (capacity — can't enforce the prose is genuine). This is upstream of both. §3.15 and §3.16 concern whether the *content* is honest and genuine; §034 concerns whether the citation is *legible* — whether the receiver was structurally denied or granted the content. A legible cite does not make the answer honest (§3.15 still bites) or the rationale genuine (§3.16 still bites). It removes the one excuse that sits before both: "I didn't know what you were pointing at." Legibility is necessary, not sufficient. The residuals survive it.

Open: how much description is enough. "Caveman succinct" is a posture, not a measure. Too little and the gloss is decoration — the ID with a label, no content transferred. Too much and the gloss becomes restatement — the cite swallows the cited, and §3.12's inheritance breaks down because the narrative now stands in for the artifact instead of pointing at it. The boundary between honest-gloss and restatement is unspecified. Genuinely open. Flag: this likely resists a measure entirely and lives as a posture the citer holds in good faith — which loops it back into §3.15 territory, where honesty can't be enforced, only practiced.
