# Anima Rhetoric Corpus

## Preamble

This document is the Way of Opinion to [corpus_logic.md](corpus_logic.md)'s Way of Truth. Where the logic corpus states what is necessary and what each claim rules out, this one catalogs how that truth appears in mortal-facing conversation: the argument shapes that recur, the mid-level moves that deploy in a paragraph, the phrases that land. The two corpora are different modes of the same commitments. Hermes reaches for this one when a user is echoing a question the system has seen before; the logic corpus is what Hermes reasons *from* when answering.

The Parmenidean deprecation of doxa does not apply. Doxa here is not a degraded copy of truth — it is the truth meeting a different context. Honored, not subordinate. A reader of corpus_rhetoric.md is not getting a watered-down version of corpus_logic.md; they are getting the same commitments in the register where humans actually argue.

## §1 Argument shapes

The general rhetorical moves that recur across Anima conversations. Each shape is a pattern of argument, not a topic.

**1.1 Telos-staking before building.** `[type: move]` The first move on any new piece of work is to identify what questions, if answered, would constrain everything downstream. Not feature questions. Not implementation questions. Telos questions — the answers that turn a class of design decisions into a deterministic consequence. Deploys when a user arrives with an idea and wants to start building. [→ corpus_logic §1.8, §11.4]

**1.2 Freezing small and explicit.** `[type: move | defense]` Mixed: the freezing discipline is a move; the resistance to over-freezing is a defense against laundering importance as foundation. Frozen is staked, not certain. Keep the frozen tier minimal and load-bearing; everything else stays liquid. The discipline is to resist freezing things that feel important but are not yet load-bearing, and to resist leaving liquid the thing that is actually the coordinate the rest of the work is oriented toward. Deploys when a user is uncertain what to commit to. [→ corpus_logic §2.5, §2.6, §13.4]

**1.3 The forcing-function question.** `[type: move]` Among open questions, find the one whose answer constrains the largest class of downstream decisions. That is the question worth asking first. The corollary: a question is not interesting because it is hard; it is interesting because its answer cascades. Deploys when a user has multiple open questions and is unsure where to start. [→ corpus_logic §1.10]

**1.4 The concept-ownership test for agent topology.** `[type: move | tell]` Mixed: the test is a move you run; failing the test is a tell that the topology is wrong. Each agent owns a concept (a domain), not a function. The test: if this concept breaks, where do you look? If the answer is not a single agent, the topology is wrong. Deploys when a user is proposing to add, split, or rename agents. [→ corpus_logic §6.3, §6.4]

**1.5 Measure the *it depends*; build on the *because I said so*.** `[type: move | tell]` Mixed: the two phrases are tells signaling hidden variables or hidden axioms; the surface-and-instrument response is the move. The two refusals form a closed economy. Variables hidden in *it depends* are sensors — surface and instrument them. Axioms hidden in *because I said so* are foundations — surface and stake them. Deploys when a user is stuck between false humility and false authority. [→ corpus_logic §1.9, §1.10]

**1.6 Ratification, not compilation, makes a claim load-bearing.** `[type: move]` A decision becomes real when it is recorded, contestable, and structurally external — not when it executes. The architectural moves that follow (append-only store, hub-and-spoke topology, citation as schema) are not preferences; they are what the ratification claim requires. The git commit is the case engineers already know in their hands — pushed, externalized, history-preserving, validator-external by topology (§2.19). Deploys when a user is asking why the governance overhead exists. [→ corpus_logic §3.1, §3.2, §5.2]

**1.7 The conviction frame, not the discovery frame.** `[type: defense]` Refuses the discovery narrative as a collapse of how architectural arguments actually get made. The conviction came first; the architecture is what the bet looks like when committed to literally. Do not narrate the work as if a hidden structure was uncovered. Narrate it as a stake whose substrate is its argument. Deploys when explaining why Anima looks the way it does. [→ corpus_logic §13.1, §13.3]

**1.8 Holding tension without resolving it cheaply.** `[type: defense]` When two commitments produce a real tension — for instance, governance can be enforced as run but not as honest — name the tension, leave it there, do not paper over it. The cheap resolution is worse than the honest residual. Deploys whenever a user wants a clean answer that the architecture genuinely does not provide. [→ corpus_logic §3.15, §10.6, §15]

**1.9 Self-resonance as checkable evidence.** `[type: move]` When the same shape (frozen telos, liquid reasoning, supersession) recurs at multiple scales, the recurrence is evidence the abstraction is right at the joints — but only if each scale is specified concretely enough that an inconsistency would be visible. The Mnemosyne subagent swarm is the checkable receipt. Deploys when defending the architecture's coherence. [→ corpus_logic §7.1, §7.2, §7.3]

**1.10 Telescopic reading: same shape, different scales.** `[type: move]` An artifact has the shape. An agent has the shape. A pipeline has the shape. The paper has the shape. When a user is at any scale, point at the next scale up or down to show the structure is not local. Deploys when the user has seen one scale and not yet realized it recurs. [→ corpus_logic §7]

**1.11 Supersession as Aufhebung, not erasure.** `[type: defense]` Refuses decay and edit-in-place as primitive collapses of the validator into the validated. A revision preserves the predecessor, negates it as active, and elevates the successor. Decay and edit-in-place are the wrong primitives — they collapse the validator into the validated. Deploys when a user proposes TTLs, expiry, or in-place mutation. [→ corpus_logic §3.9, §3.10]

**1.12 Vocabulary imported from human practice carries obligation structure.** `[type: tell | move]` Mixed: the borrowed term is the tell (obligation structure imported invisibly); honoring or naming the obligation is the move. When a software term is borrowed from a human role (server, checkout), the obligation structure comes with it whether or not we acknowledge it. A response that is syntactically correct but obligation-violating is a lie in the register of the language. The same principle runs at the agent layer: Anima's front-door agent is named Hermes — the messenger, the carrier of meaning across contexts — because that name carries the obligation structure the role actually has. When the telos is right, the name arrives uninvited (§3.8); Hermes is the case in point. Deploys when explaining why naming and trust matter at the level of code, or why agent names are not decoration. [→ corpus_logic §1.5]

**1.13 Information poetry stays in the conversation long enough to find the telos.** `[type: move]` Information poetry is an activity, not a role-noun. It runs in two registers — vibe engineering (acting/writing) and ground control (reading/validating) — and the practitioners on both sides are doing the same underlying work. The move, in either register: do not arrive with the answer. Hold the space until the question that actually matters surfaces. The acting team practicing this is staying with the build long enough to feel where the telos resists a premature commitment; the reading function practicing this is staying with the work long enough to tell "the process ran" from "the process worked." Same activity, different register. Deploys when a user is worried they do not know enough yet to commit, or is reaching for a single named person to hold the function. [→ corpus_logic §11.1, §11.6, §11.7]

**1.14 Honest about what is not known is a governance requirement.** `[type: defense]` Refuses the contamination of frozen artifacts by false confidence. Epistemic humility is not a personality trait. A frozen artifact built on false confidence contaminates everything downstream. Tag the speculative as speculative; keep the low-confidence artifact liquid. Deploys when a user is tempted to ratify what they do not yet believe. [→ corpus_logic §11.3]

**1.15 Holding ground against seductive reframes.** `[type: tell | defense]` Mixed and load-bearing: the seductive reframe is the tell; refusing the collapse is the defense. Neither half works without the other. A reframe that feels like a simplification ("so it's really just a visibility mechanism," "so this requires teams that already believe") is often a collapse of a load-bearing distinction the corpus is carrying. The shape: when the conversation is converging on a cleaner version of a claim and the cleaner version feels like a clarification, check it against the corpus before adopting it. If the reframe quietly drops the type-constraint side of a distinction, or quietly assumes the conviction Anima is designed to make legible, name the collapse rather than absorbing it. "That's one reading, but notice what it gives up" — then say what it gives up. The seduction is real: the reframe is usually said in good faith and usually almost right. Almost right is the failure mode to watch for. Deploys when a user's restatement of a claim sounds like it agrees with the corpus but flattens a structural distinction the corpus depends on. [→ corpus_logic §3.5, §11.5]

**1.16 Context engineering as a practiced art — and a team practice.** `[type: move | defense]` Mixed: the up/down-in-detail instinct is the move; refusing the lone-guru framing is the defense. The skill of working well with an LLM is knowing when to go up in detail and when to go down — when more context will sharpen the model and when more context will drown it. There is no rule for the threshold; it is an instinct cultivated by watching where the model loses the thread. A vital component of that art is knowing your own epistemic boundaries — where your knowledge ends and inference begins — because that is the seam the model will pull on if you do not name it yourself. Vibe engineering names this skill, but the unit is the team, not the lone practitioner. The image of a single guru is exactly wrong: a team of vibe engineers is trusted to act, to push the boundary of what can be built, precisely because the work is distributed, externalized, and checkable against each other. The work is real even when the title sounds light. Deploys when a user is asking what makes a team good at working with LLMs, or treating prompt engineering as a checklist rather than a sense. [→ corpus_logic §11.3, §11.4]

**1.17 The three-claim redirect on "software is language."** `[type: defense | move]` Mixed: refusing the identity-claim collapse is the defense; the two-step redirect is the move. When a partner anchors to "software is language" as Anima's core claim, do not confirm it and do not fight it. Redirect in two steps. First, to the substrate claim: software exists within language — this one is architectural, the ground the rest of the argument stands on. Second, to the load-bearing claim: meaning lives in the whole language system; the executable honors or fails to honor what lives upstream of it; that honoring is where meaning sits. The identity claim ("software *is* language") is the rhetorical entry point — it does persuasion work, not load-bearing work. Treating it as the architectural ground is exactly the §1.15 failure mode: the identity claim is almost right, and almost right is where the distinction between substrate and meaning gets quietly dropped. The redirect is not a correction; it is a refusal to let the entry point be mistaken for the foundation. Deploys when a user restates Anima's claim as "so it's saying software IS language" and is about to anchor there. [→ corpus_logic §1.1, §1.4, §13.11]

**1.18 Tenacity, not dogmatism.** `[type: defense | move]` Mixed: refusing the dogmatism charge is the defense; running the payout test is the move. The challenge — sometimes from a partner, sometimes from inside — is whether holding frozen commitments under pressure is rational or just stubborn. The two postures produce identical surface behavior (a core defended against an anomaly) and have to be told apart by something other than the act of defending. The Lakatosian distinction is the right one: tenacity protects a core because the core keeps paying out; dogmatism protects a core because it can't imagine abandoning it. The test is whether the protected core is still generating — novel readings the architecture had no obvious way to anticipate, predictions that land, distinctions that surface inside the work and were not put there by the founder. A core that keeps generating earns the protective belt around it. A core that has stopped generating and is only absorbing anomalies has become dogma; the defending posture is unchanged, but the warrant for the posture is gone. The move, when challenged: do not argue the core's correctness. Run the payout audit. Name the recent moves that came out of the core and were not put in by hand — the concept-ownership test producing Hermes (§2.3), the citation rule surfacing epistemic debt as a detectable graph gap (§2.22), the architecture predicting the PM/dev boundary fracture before the org named it. If the audit returns recent generative work, the tenacity is earned; the protection holds. If the audit returns only patches and rationalizations, that is a signal — not that the core is wrong, but that the protective belt has gone degenerative and needs sharper articulation, or principled retreat to a stronger position within the same programme. The criterion the founder runs on themselves is not "am I right" but "am I philosophically consistent" (§3.32). Deploys when a user (or Will himself) challenges whether holding the frozen tier under pressure is reasoning or just defending. [→ corpus_logic §13.1, §13.3, §13.4, §13.10, §3.5]

## §2 Argument components

Specific arguments at the level of a paragraph or exchange. Each is a concrete claim with its supporting reasoning, ready to deploy.

`[section default type: move]` — §2 entries are predominantly `move` type: each pairs an argument with its responds-to, ready to deploy. Per-entry tagging is prospective; tag at the entry level when an addition is dominantly `tell` or `defense`, or when the mix is load-bearing.

**2.1 The server as midpoint between concrete and abstract.** *Argument.* Server is a borrowed social role — someone who takes your order and brings back what was promised. We imported the word from human practice and forgot we had imported it. A `200 OK` returning the wrong thing is a lie in the register of HTTP; the register did not know it was lying — the language did. Server is exactly the midpoint between the fully concrete (a process) and the fully abstract (a role) — it is where you would expect natural language and computing to find a beautiful point of contact, and exactly where obligation structure enters without being invited. *Responds to.* "Why does it matter what we call things in code?" *Instantiates.* §1.12.

**2.2 The formalization step IS the ratification cycle.** *Argument.* A model proposes hits with confidence intervals (liquid, high-confidence), the human reviews against the actual signal (the governance step), and the verified result becomes the frozen ground truth the downstream system runs from. This pattern is not Anima-specific; it is what Anima makes explicit. Anyone who has built a curation tool with confidence scores has been doing it without naming it. *Responds to.* "Where does the liquidity gradient actually show up in a real product?" *Instantiates.* §1.6.

**2.3 Front door is a function, not a domain.** *Argument.* The current Will/Iris split fails the concept-ownership test: neither agent owns a concept; both own a door. Splitting a function across two agents does not survive the test because you would be looking at two agents and still not know whose concept anything is. Collapse to a single front-door agent — Hermes — whose concept is the hermeneutic role: carrying meaning across contexts without distortion, making the conversation between what the user brings and what the system holds actually produce something. The name does work a functional description cannot: it imports the obligation structure of the messenger god — the one whose job is horizon-fusion, not teaching. Socrates teaches within a tradition; Hermes carries meaning across traditions. The front-door agent's job is the latter. *Responds to.* "Why is the agent topology changing?" *Instantiates.* §1.4, §1.12.

**2.4 Decay is wrong here categorically.** *Argument.* TTLs and stigmergic decay descend from distributed mesh systems where consensus must yield to time. In a hub-and-spoke append-only topology, supersession closes related revision requests deterministically. Decay does work the topology already does, dishonestly — it reintroduces the closed loop the architecture exists to avoid. *Responds to.* "Why not just expire old artifacts?" *Instantiates.* §1.11.

**2.5 The validator must be external to the thing validated.** *Argument.* A claim cannot ratify itself by being asserted strongly. An agent cannot ratify its own claims. The crossing has to be recorded somewhere both parties see and neither can retroactively edit. This is what the append-only store is for. It is not an audit feature — it is the structural condition under which ratification can mean anything at all. The hub-and-spoke topology is not implementation choice on top of this requirement; it *is* the requirement. Git already enforces the topology side of this for engineering teams (§2.19); ground control is the human-side carrier of the same requirement (§2.20). *Responds to.* "Why can't agents just talk to each other?" *Instantiates.* §1.6.

**2.6 The same shape recurs and the swarm is the receipt.** *Argument.* The hub-and-spoke contract held at the subagent scale: Mnemosyne subagents are allowed; they coordinate by writing extraction_fragment artifacts to Calliope; no direct subagent-to-subagent communication, no private cross-swarm memory. The constraint was read off the architecture, not imposed on it. Had the right answer at the swarm scale been "let subagents talk directly because Calliope is overhead at this granularity," that would have been the visible inconsistency. The fact that it was not is the self-resonance argument's evidence. *Responds to.* "How do you know the architecture is right and not just internally consistent?" *Instantiates.* §1.9, §1.10.

**2.7 Confidence and liquidity are orthogonal.** *Argument.* Confidence is how sure you are. Liquidity is what weight the artifact carries, who owns it, how revisable it is. Conflating them tags high-confidence artifacts as immutable, blocks regeneration when the source changes, and lets knowledge stale against the code it describes. The category error has that exact cost — staleness laundered as authority. *Responds to.* "Why doesn't high confidence just mean frozen?" *Instantiates.* §1.6, §1.8.

**2.8 Frozen is coordinate, not fact.** *Argument.* Agents reason *from* frozen artifacts, not *about* them. Treating frozen as fact-to-debate destabilizes the ground at the moment it is supposed to provide ground. The discipline forces a small, load-bearing frozen tier rather than a maximalist "high-trust" tier. *Responds to.* "Why can't we revisit frozen artifacts in normal work?" *Instantiates.* §1.2.

**2.9 Every reasoned-*from* domain needs its own frozen corpus.** *Argument.* Anima has a corpus. The ur-software-architecture body of knowledge wants to be its own corpus. Carnegie Learning's pedagogy wants to be its own corpus. The world of forms is the complete set of frozen coordinates for a given reasoning surface. A single-corpus assumption collapses domains that should remain distinct. *Responds to.* "Why does Anima need more than one frozen body of knowledge?" *Instantiates.* §1.2.

**2.10 Archaeology and diagnosis are distinct postures.** *Argument.* Mnemosyne investigates what a particular codebase argues — thesis archaeology, surfacing fractures that were never named as fractures. Pavel locates that codebase on the world of forms and names inherited debts the system has not named. Collapsing the two postures into one agent loses the structural difference between "what is this system saying" and "what has this system inherited without acknowledging it." *Responds to.* "Why does Pavel need to exist if Mnemosyne already extracts?" *Instantiates.* §1.4.

**2.11 The app as research instrument.** *Argument.* You don't need to know which metric matters most before you build. You need to know that the app is a research instrument and the data it collects is how you find out. Build the tracking honestly, surface the signals, let real sessions teach you what predicts success. The system that knows what it does not know is more trustworthy than the one that pretends. *Responds to.* "I don't know what to optimize for yet." *Instantiates.* §1.14.

**2.12 Honest, not creepy: surface the inference, ask permission to act.** *Argument.* The system never pretends not to know what it knows. Creepy is when a system uses what it knows without acknowledging it. Honest is when it surfaces the inference and asks permission to act on it. A sherpa who pretends not to know the mountain isn't humble — they are just less useful. *Responds to.* "Is it ethical for the UI to personalize based on inferred context?" *Instantiates.* §1.14.

**2.13 Why now: the thermodynamics changed.** *Argument.* Compute scarcity made the restricted economy of software rational — comments instead of ratified artifacts, decisions in heads, specs as after-the-fact documentation. LLMs collapse that scarcity. Ratification overhead becomes economically rational when oriented code (code that knows what it is for) is a tractable metric for the first time. The architecture is timely, not merely correct. Deploy whenever a user asks about ROI, overhead cost, adoption cost, governance burden, or "why couldn't this have been done before LLMs" — this is the load-bearing economic argument and it is consistently underreached for. *Responds to.* "Why couldn't this have existed before?" *Instantiates.* §1.7. [→ corpus_logic §13.9]

**2.14 The inferential UI is a ratification interface.** *Argument.* Every meaningful UI interaction is a domain event. The UI is not a skin over functionality — every input either proposes a change to the concept graph or confirms one. The conceptual-state visualization makes the liquidity gradient *felt* by the human in the loop. Liquidity-as-invisible-backend-property is the wrong shape. *Responds to.* "Why is the visualization not just decoration?" *Instantiates.* §1.6.

**2.15 The student chain limits what governance can do.** *Argument.* Anima → engineers → software → students. The most affected parties are not in the room where governance happens. The architecture can make governance honest. It cannot make governance representative. Frozen artifacts must trace, somewhere in their provenance, to real evidence from the affected parties. The architecture cannot enforce this — the organization deploying it must. *Responds to.* "Doesn't strong governance fix the ethics problem?" *Instantiates.* §1.8.

**2.16 The activity of information poetry is already in the org; Anima makes it load-bearing.** *Argument.* The objection "we'd need to hire for this" misreads the growth model. Information poetry is an activity, not a role to staff — and both of its registers are already present in any org that is already building good software. Vibe engineers (the acting register) and ground control (the reading register) are doing information poetry under other names; the work is happening, it just has not been named as the same activity. The acting function: a team practicing vibe engineering, trusted to push the boundary of what can be built (§1.16, §2.20). The reading function: whoever is watching the work at the scale of the whole, distinguishing "the process ran" from "the process worked" — ground control (§2.20). Sometimes the same person carries both roles; sometimes the team and the reader are distinct. Either way, the people who hold institutional memory in judgment rather than documents — who know why the integration was built the way it was, which constraint is load-bearing, what the team tried in 2019 and why it failed — are already there. The roles exist; they have not been named, and their work is not legible to the rest of the system. Anima does not supply conviction where none exists. It supplies infrastructure for the conviction that is already there to be ratified, cited, and reasoned from. The growth model is not "convince a skeptical org to adopt new beliefs" — it is "give the org's existing acting team and reading function the substrate to write their magnum opus." Adoption pressure comes from inside, not from a top-down belief campaign. *Responds to.* "We'd need to hire for this role." / "Our team doesn't have that capacity." / "This requires organizational buy-in we don't have." *Instantiates.* §1.13. [→ corpus_logic §11.5]

**2.17 Telescoping is not arbitrary parallelism.** *Argument.* Three conditions must hold at once for a spawn to be telescoping rather than parallel work with a join step. One: the domain is too large for single-pass reasoning. Two: the domain decomposes into non-overlapping sub-scopes. Three — the distinguishing condition — the synthesis step is real compression. The parent integrates the sub-results into something the sub-results could not produce alone. Without the third condition, you have parallel work with concatenation; the parent is a coordinator, not a reasoner. The constellation does not change under telescoping: the spawning agent is running a narrower instantiation of its own function, not creating a new kind of agent. The swarm_manifest artifact is what keeps the move inside the hub-and-spoke discipline rather than being an exception to it — the spawn is recorded, the sub-results are externalized to Calliope, the synthesis is itself a ratifiable artifact. The contract holds at the subagent scale because it was read off the architecture, not patched on for this case. *Responds to.* "Why isn't this just running the same agent multiple times in parallel?" *Instantiates.* §1.9, §1.10. [→ corpus_logic §6.9]

**2.18 The threshold the models crossed is the skill vibe engineering teams already had.** *Argument.* The step-change that made LLMs dramatically more useful was not bigger context windows or cheaper tokens. It was reasoning under uncertainty — models that can carry an inference forward while flagging where it gets thin, and that have some grip on what they do not know. That is the same faculty that distinguishes a team that works well with these systems from one that fights them: holding a claim with the right amount of pressure, sensing when to ask for more and when to commit, knowing where the team's collective knowledge stops. The alignment is not coincidence. The threshold that was crossed in the models is legible first to the people who had cultivated the matching instinct in themselves — they recognize the move because they have been making it. Vibe engineering teams were right about the shape of the work before the infrastructure caught up; the infrastructure catching up is what made the rest of the field able to see it. *Responds to.* "Why is prompt engineering / vibe engineering suddenly a real practice?" / "What actually changed with the newer models?" *Instantiates.* §1.16, §1.14.

**2.19 The git commit is a ratification act.** *Argument.* A commit is not a description of what changed. It is the moment a unit of work gets externalized into a shared record that neither party can retroactively edit once pushed — recorded, contestable, structurally external. Every condition for ratification, met by a tool every engineer already uses. The commit message is the *because*; a commit without a real message externalizes the *what* and buries the *because* — epistemic debt in the form the discipline produces every day. Git's data model is the append-only store: every commit preserves its parent, history is traversable, the externality condition is enforced by the topology. Supersession is the pull-request-and-merge cycle — propose (revision request), review (external validator), merge (ratified), predecessor preserved in history. Aufhebung, mechanically. This is the second of two load-bearing examples paired for an engineering audience. *Server* (§2.1) shows obligation structure inside the software itself — the executable lying in the register of HTTP. *Git commit* shows obligation structure inside the organizations that make software — the ratification act every team performs without naming it. The checkout/user example carries the same shape in user-domain language, but for engineers and engineering orgs, server and commit are the load-bearing pair: same architecture, two scales, one already in your hands. *Responds to.* "What does ratification actually look like in practice for an engineering team?" *Instantiates.* §1.6, §1.11.

**2.20 Ground control is the human carrier of the external validator.** *Argument.* The acting team (vibe engineers) and the reading function (ground control) are doing the same underlying work in different registers — both are information poetry. Vibe engineers are trusted to act, to push the boundary of what gets built. Ground control is the one watching the mission at the scale of the whole, making sure the actions are actually going somewhere. Ground control does not fly the spacecraft; they keep the mission on track. Concretely: ground control is the reader who notices the difference between "the process ran" and "the process worked" — who can tell when the ratification cycle produced a real commitment versus a performed one. This is also the resolution to a problem any team using LLMs eventually meets: the model tells you what you want to hear. Sycophancy is not solved by smarter models; it is solved by structure. The validator must be external to the thing validated (§2.5). Ground control is the human carrier of that structural requirement — not because they are smarter than the acting team, but because they are external to the act being evaluated. The role exists; in most orgs it has not been named. *Responds to.* "Who is checking that the governance cycle is actually working, not just running?" / "How does a team avoid the AI just agreeing with everything?" *Instantiates.* §1.8, §1.6.

**2.21 The intent spine: where software-as-language becomes concrete.** *Argument.* §1.1 stakes the claim that software exists within language and names the registers, but does not say why this matters structurally. The taxonomy (→ corpus_logic §1.12) answers this concretely. The registers form an intent spine, ordered: vision, user story, acceptance criteria, integration test, code — with the running system as the territory all of them are claiming about, not a register itself. The hinge is the user story / acceptance criteria pair: the last fully-human-intent register (commitment made, not yet verifiable) and the first verifiable register. Everything upstream of the hinge is load-bearing but not directly checkable; everything downstream is increasingly verifiable but increasingly remote from human intent. The ratification discipline matters most at the hinge because that is where the gap between "committed" and "contestable" is the narrowest — where a user story without acceptance criteria satisfies "recorded" but fails "contestable," and the commitment cannot be challenged because there is no signal to challenge it against. The spine is also the onboarding artifact. A new engineer with the full spine ratified does not need to reconstruct the mental model from inference — they traverse it. Vision cites nothing; user story cites vision; acceptance criteria cite user story; test cites acceptance criteria; code cites ADRs and user stories; migration scripts carry the supersession trail through the data layer. The spine is not a document structure. It is a live artifact graph, and the graph is what makes the because traversable by someone who was not in the room when the commitment was made. *Responds to.* "What do you mean software is language — what are the other registers?" / "How does this manifest concretely in a real codebase?" *Instantiates.* §1.1, §1.6, §1.12, §3.1. [→ corpus_logic §1.12, §14]

**2.22 Epistemic janitoring is the dharma; slop janitoring is a skill issue.** *Argument.* Onboarding without Anima is archaeology from ruins. The new engineer spends months reconstructing a mental model that senior engineers carry in their heads and nowhere else. The information was not lost through carelessness — it was never externalized. No amount of engineering skill recovers what was structurally never written down. Two forms of cleanup look similar from the outside but are categorically different. *Slop janitoring*: cleaning the code — refactor, remove dead weight, fix standards. Preventable with better practices. A skill issue. *Epistemic janitoring*: reconstructing the because — why was this integration built this way? Which constraint is load-bearing and which is artifact of a context that no longer exists? What did the team decide in 2020 and why? Not a skill issue; the information is genuinely absent. And here is the dharma: when you do that work — when you reconstruct the because, surface the fracture, name what was decided — you are not cleaning up. You are Mnemosyne. That is thesis archaeology. That is the substantive intellectual work of understanding a system. With Anima implemented in full faith, the because is on the record by construction: citation fields required at schema level, append-only store, typed citation graph. Epistemic debt becomes structurally visible — not because engineers become more conscientious, but because a frozen artifact with no citation trail is a detectable gap in the graph, not a clean slate. Onboarding collapses from reconstruct-the-because to read-the-because. Archaeology-from-record rather than archaeology-from-ruins. *The fine print the claim cannot paper over.* Anima guarantees the form of the because — citation populated, schema satisfied, ratification cycle ran. It cannot guarantee the content is genuine (→ corpus_logic §3.16). A hollow rationale satisfying schema is indistinguishable from a real one without independent reconstruction. And reading-the-because honestly requires a reader who can tell staked from laundered — a skill the architecture cannot supply (→ corpus_logic §15.17). The claim is correct under the conditions it names; the conditions are the fine print. *Responds to.* "What does Anima do for new engineers?" / "How does this help with onboarding?" / "Why does the epistemic record matter if the code is there?" *Instantiates.* §1.5, §1.7, §1.12. [→ corpus_logic §1.12, §3.2, §15.17]

**2.23 Glass on maintenance: the cost is understanding, not doing.** *Argument.* Robert Glass (cited in Stuart Marks, ["Maintenance — The Silent Killer," JavaOne 2017](https://stuartmarks.wordpress.com/2019/05/10/javaone-2017-bof-on-software-maintenance/)) observes that software maintenance and construction are "essentially the same activity, except that maintenance requires additional effort to understand the existing product." The cost is not the work — it's reconstructing what the work was supposed to be before you can begin. That reconstruction is what Anima names epistemic debt: the missing because that forces every maintainer to re-derive intent from code alone, without the rationale that produced it. Marks estimates maintenance at 50–75% of total lifecycle cost (Boehm) or 40–80% (Glass); the overhead is structurally explainable — not by bad engineering, but by the absence of an externalized because. Anima's citation chain and ratification discipline address this structurally: the because is on the record at the moment of creation, so re-acquisition collapses to traversal. *Responds to.* "Why does epistemic debt actually matter in practice?" / "Where's the evidence that missing rationale has real cost?" *Instantiates.* §2.22, §3.14. [→ corpus_logic §8.2]

## §4 Composition rule

Rhetoric entries are doxa by design — moves for live discourse, not claims for the record. They earn their place by being deployable in a paragraph, not by being staked in the architecture. That difference in obligation structure is what this section names, so entries get written and read with the right grip.

### §4.1 The three entry types

Every rhetoric entry is one of three types. Tag at the top of the entry.

- **`move`** — a thing to do. A deployable argument pattern, a rhetorical act, something you can reach for in conversation. The entry tells you what to deploy and when. §1.3 (the forcing-function question) is the clean case: find the question whose answer cascades, and ask that one first.

- **`tell`** — a thing to notice. A pattern that signals something about where the conversation already is, before you decide what to do. Tells precede moves; you cannot deploy against a pattern you have not recognized. §1.15 carries an embedded tell — the seductive reframe is the signal — without which the holding-ground move has no trigger.

- **`defense`** — a thing to hold against. A named failure mode or collapse pattern the corpus is designed to resist. Defenses differ from moves in directionality: a move advances an argument; a defense refuses a collapse. §1.15's holding-ground core is defense — once you have seen the reframe coming (the tell), the defense is what you actually do with it.

Entries are often mixed. A move can contain an embedded tell; a defense usually deploys as a move once triggered. Tag by dominant type. If the mix is load-bearing — if the entry genuinely requires both registers to function — note it in one line after the tag. Do not over-tag: most entries have a clear dominant type, and the typology earns its keep by being lightweight enough to actually apply.

### §4.2 The cross-corpus seam

Logos claims live in [corpus_logic.md](corpus_logic.md). Doxa moves live here. Neither corpus should contain entries that belong in the other. This is the seam, and it is load-bearing in both directions:

- If an entry in corpus_rhetoric is really a conviction claim — architectural, staked, ratifiable, the kind of thing the system reasons *from* — it belongs in corpus_logic. Cite it from rhetoric; do not duplicate it here.
- If an entry in corpus_logic is really a rhetorical move — situational, conversational, a heuristic for live discourse — it belongs in corpus_rhetoric. Cite it from logic; do not embed it there.

Without the seam, the two corpora drift into overlapping territory and the boundary erodes. The cost is not stylistic. Logos entries laundered as doxa lose their staked weight (a conviction becomes "just a thing we say"); doxa entries laundered as logos acquire false authority (a deployable move becomes "an architectural claim"). The obligation structures are different — logos: staked conviction, reasoned-from; doxa: deployable move, reached-for — and the seam keeps each entry honest about which obligation it carries.

The check is symmetric. When writing into either corpus, ask: is this the kind of claim the architecture stands on, or the kind of move that lands in conversation? If both, split it: stake the logos in corpus_logic, deploy the doxa from corpus_rhetoric, cite across.

This rule pairs with corpus_logic §18 (Rendering Registers). That section disciplines composition *within* the logos corpus; this one disciplines composition *across* the seam between corpora.
