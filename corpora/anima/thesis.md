# Teleological Software

Software development is a language game between rational agents. It always has been.

Wittgenstein's point about language is that meaning is use embedded in shared practice — a word means what it does in the activity that deploys it, governed by rules that are part explicit and part tacit. Software is this without remainder. A requirement, a spec, an implementation, a test: each is a move in a game whose rules the players mostly know and partly only feel. The type signature of `checkout` does not fix what `checkout` means. What fixes it is what happens when it runs, what the caller expected, what the user thought they were doing — the use, spanning registers, embedded in the practice of a team. The frozen commitments that team plays from — what counts as done, what the system is for, which invariants are not up for renegotiation — are its form of life in Wittgenstein's exact sense: the bedrock priors the game is operated from rather than proved within it. Teams have always had these. They have mostly kept them in heads.

This is not a new claim about software. It is a recognition of what software already was before anyone named it. Teams have always played this game; they have mostly played it without admitting it was a game, which is why the rules stayed tacit and the *because* stayed in heads. Anima's contribution is not to invent the game. It is to make the players explicit, assign each to the stance its work actually occupies, and build the contracts that let the game proceed honestly rather than by inheritance and lucky memory.

---

Software has a telos as a constitutive cause, and that is what the title means. Aristotle's four causes are the right tool because the claim is ontological — about what software *is*, not about how an observer predicts it. The material cause is the substrate: bytes, instructions, the running process. The efficient cause is what brings it into being: the team, now the agents. The formal cause is the structure: the architecture, the spec, the shape the thing takes. The final cause is what it is for — the staked end. Aristotle's claim, and the one this thesis runs on, is that the final cause is primary: remove the telos and the other three lose direction. Substrate becomes noise, the efficient cause becomes exertion, form becomes shape. Software is not a thing that happens to have a purpose laid over it. The purpose is the cause that orders the rest, which is why a system built to run toward no staked end is not a leaner version of the same thing — it is a different and lesser kind of thing.

Dennett's three stances do a different job, and conflating the two frames is the error to avoid. The stances are epistemic — predictive postures an observer adopts — not claims about what a system is. They earn their place here because they explain why the agent topology looks the way it does: each agent operates at the stance that matches its actual work. Compiled code is predicted from the physical stance — deterministic, no interpretation, a hedged bet compiled to be measured in the field. Hermes, the front-door agent, operates at the intentional stance: human intent arrives in natural language, the most expressive instrument a person has for externalizing a mental state, and Hermes meets it with context — corpus, prior commitments, conversation history. Meaning is not in the utterance and not in Hermes. It emerges in the loop between them — Gadamer's fusion of horizons, running in production. Hephaestus, the implementer, operates at the design stance: by the time a story card reaches Heph with ratified acceptance criteria, the interpretive work is done, and Heph's job is fulfillment of purpose, no malfunction assumed. The stances do not constitute the architecture. They explain why three positions that could have been one are correctly kept distinct.

Ratification is the operation between two of those stances. It is how intentional-stance content — Hermes's interpretation, still contestable — becomes a design-stance commitment Heph can build from. The governance cycle is not overhead laid on the system. It is what the epistemic situation requires the moment you take the stances seriously instead of letting them blur.

---

The restricted economy of software is what the game becomes when the players refuse to name it, and in Dennett's terms it is precise: the three stances collapsed into one. Code is ground truth, specs are reference docs, rationale is a comment block if you are lucky. Everything intentional-stance — beliefs, interpretations, the reasons a choice was made — got flattened into a single physical-stance artifact, the executable, and the design-stance layer in between was left implicit, recoverable only by archaeology.

This was rational. Producing every register — the why alongside the what alongside the how — cost more than teams could sustain, so they dropped the expensive ones and called it shipping. The choice was correct under the constraint. LLMs collapsed the constraint. Producing all three registers is now cheap enough that a system still running the restricted economy is leaving recoverable meaning on the floor. That is not a productivity story. It is an epistemics story, and the failed onboardings, the untouchable systems, and the decisions made twice are its symptoms, not its substance. The *because* lived in heads and left when the heads left, because nothing in the game required it to be written down. Now something can.

---

A decision becomes real when ratified, not when compiled, and ratification means three things at once: recorded, contestable, structurally external. Recorded without contestable is logging. Contestable without external is consensus — ratification by the loudest voice. The externality condition is the load-bearing one, and it forces the architecture. An append-only store no party can retroactively edit is the structural condition for external. A hub-and-spoke topology, in which every agent reads and writes that store and none speaks directly to another, *is* the externality requirement made topological — the store is the place outside every agent. Remove the spoke and you remove the externality; ratification becomes ceremony. This is also where the hardest case is survived. The threat to externality is not the outside skeptic but the founder, who knows every seam and can reframe an anomaly as a clarification to exempt his own corpus. An external store he cannot retroactively edit is what bites at exactly that point. Most frameworks collapse there because nothing structural stops the founder; here the topology does.

The liquidity gradient is Lakatos made structural. Frozen artifacts are the hard core — methodologically protected, reasoned *from*, the commitments that define what the programme is; they are the form of life the game is played from. Liquid artifacts are the protective belt — revisable auxiliary hypotheses, specific interpretations and approaches. When tests fail or contradictions surface, the belt is revised and the core is not. The gradient is not an arbitrary stability ranking. It is the shape of a healthy research programme. Two axes govern it, and conflating them is the standard failure: confidence (how sure) and liquidity (what weight, who owns, how revisable) are independent. Conflate them and you launder staleness as authority — freeze a claim because you are confident, block its regeneration, let it stale against the code it describes. Supersession, never deletion, keeps the belt's revision history legible.

One discipline follows directly: separate belief from truth. *Hermes owns X* asserts a ratified commitment. *We believe Hermes should own X* records a working hypothesis still under test. The two are different epistemic acts, and writing the second as if it were the first is the restricted economy's collapse reappearing inside the system built to refuse it — intentional-stance content laundered into design-stance authority. Keeping them distinct is what lets the corpus track its own belief revision: the Lakatosian record of a programme that knows what it has staked and what it is still testing.

---

The strongest objection arrives here. Hicks and colleagues argue that LLMs are Frankfurtian bullshitters: not liars, who know the truth and violate it, but systems with no orientation toward truth at all. The charge has force against an LLM emitting prose. It fails against Heph in code space, because the charge assumes a theory of truth this architecture refuses.

Frankfurt's bullshit is indifference to how things really are — correspondence to an external reality. This architecture does not run on correspondence. A claim's truth here is what survives contestation: survival against the append-only store and the agents entitled to contest it, not match to an external reality no party in the system can directly check. A system running a pragmatic theory of truth is not failing the correspondence test Frankfurt sets; it is declining to be measured by it. The charge does not dissolve — it lands on a different target than the one Anima presents.

I should be exact about the status of the behavioral claim, because the discipline demands it. *Heph exhibits truth-seeking* is an empirical claim under the intentional stance — working theory, falsifiable in the field, not ratified doctrine. The thesis eats its own dogfood here: it stays labeled as a belief rather than written as commitment. The same discipline governs the next claim. *Heph follows the Gricean maxims relative to its belief state* is also intentional-stance theory, not observed fact, and it is held as one.

Held that way, Grice still recovers what the bullshit frame erases. A claim about what the code does carries a sincerity condition — the assertion presents Heph as believing it correct — and when Heph is wrong, the failure has a determinate shape. It is a Quality violation from thin evidence, a Relation violation from a wrong task model, or a Quantity violation from over-specification, and each demands a different design response. Bullshit collapses all three into one shrug. Heph misreading a spec is honest error under a theory, not indifference to truth, and the distinction governs which intervention is correct.

What this is not, stated plainly. Not a record system: event sourcing achieves recorded and queryable, never contestable and externally validated. Not a correctness engine: Anima does not verify that code corresponds to some external truth; its truth is what survives contestation, and formal methods sit outside the running system where they cannot be ratified, superseded, or cited from within. Not code-as-source: the spec is authoritative, and a pipeline mining intent back out of code has already conceded the premise Anima exists to refuse. Not process-without-purpose: remove the telos and the final cause goes with it, and effort becomes exertion, pattern becomes shape, a fully instrumented cycle running toward no staked end — which is to say, by Aristotle's count, a different and lesser kind of thing.

The game was always being played. Anima makes the players explicit, assigns each to the stance its work occupies, and builds the contracts that let the game proceed honestly. The *because* is recoverable now. The only open question is whether you build the infrastructure to recover it.
